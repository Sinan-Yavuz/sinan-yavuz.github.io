Growth Models of Achievement: Maximum Likelihood vs. Bayesian Estimation

How fast do children's math skills grow from kindergarten to fifth grade, and what predicts that growth? My PhD thesis fit a three-level quadratic growth model to ECLS-K 2011 data with maximum likelihood and with Bayesian estimation, including priors built from the earlier ECLS-K 1998 cohort. With the full sample the methods agree. With a small sample, prior information starts to matter, both when it's right and when it's wrong. Try it below.

Thesis

Investigating Growth Models of Academic Achievement Scores by Maximum Likelihood and Bayesian Estimations

Sinan Yavuz · PhD in Educational Measurement and Evaluation, Hacettepe University, Ankara · 2021 · Advisor: Prof. Dr. Selahattin Gelbal · Written in Turkish

A quadratic growth model of math achievement from kindergarten to grade 5 (nine time points; 2,181 students in 165 schools), with motivation and attention as time-varying covariates, gender, ethnicity, school starting age and SES at the student level, and school type and free-lunch share at the school level. Both methods gave similar results. Bayesian estimation had slightly lower prediction error in the full sample and in a 15-school subsample, though the differences were in the second or third decimal place.

Maximum likelihood standard practice

Uses only the current data. Close to unbiased, but with few schools its estimates swing widely from sample to sample.

Bayes, noninformative no prior information

Very wide priors, so the data speak alone. Its fixed-effect estimates matched maximum likelihood closely in the thesis; in this demo they match exactly (see the notes below).

Bayes, informative priors this research

Priors taken from an earlier cohort's estimates, with the thesis's prior SDs from ECLS-K 1998. Their variance can be inflated for safety.

Scenarios:

The thesis used 165, then a random 15 for its small-sample analysis.

Each student is measured at 9 time points.

Systematic shift of every prior mean, in prior SDs. At 0 the earlier cohort matches this one, apart from its own sampling error.

Applied to every prior here. The thesis multiplied only its time-related priors' variance by 10, since the cohorts were tested at different time points.

Substantively, math scores grow fast early and slow down later. Motivation, attention, later school entry and higher SES all predict higher scores. Boys and girls start at about the same level, but boys grow faster at first and plateau sooner. Black and Hispanic students start lower than Asian and White students and grow more slowly early on, and their growth levels off later. School type affects only the curvature of growth.

About this demo

Each replication simulates students in schools from the thesis's model, measured at kindergarten fall and spring, grade 1 and 2 fall and spring, and grades 3–5 spring (time points 0–5, 7, 9, 11). True effects and variance components come from the thesis's full-sample estimates; prior SDs come from its ECLS-K 1998 priors. Covariates are simplified to gender, SES (standardized) and public vs. private school (93% public, as in the thesis sample). The school-level variance of growth rates isn't reported in the thesis, so the demo assumes a small value (0.10). Time is counted in half-years from kindergarten fall, so "growth rate at K entry" is the linear term of the quadratic, not the average growth over the years.

In each replication the informative prior is centered on a fresh draw around the truth, with the prior SD as its spread, the way an estimate from an earlier cohort would vary. The drift slider adds a systematic shift on top. A prior centered exactly on the truth would make informative priors look better than they could be in practice.

To run in your browser, estimation is two-stage: a growth curve per student, then a GLS regression of each growth coefficient on the covariates with a school random effect. The Bayesian versions combine that likelihood with normal priors; the noninformative one uses priors with SD 10,000. Both methods plug in the same estimates of the variance components instead of averaging over their uncertainty, so noninformative Bayes reproduces maximum likelihood exactly. In the full Bayesian model of the thesis, it gave nearly the same fixed effects, but different variance components and somewhat wider intervals with few schools (Tables 17 and 18). This approximates the full three-level model's fixed effects (the thesis used lme4 and brms/Stan). Results here illustrate how the methods behave; they aren't the thesis's results. Each run uses 200 replications.
