SABDB: Small Area Bayesian Dynamic Borrowing

Large-scale assessments like NAEP and PISA sample enough students to report state averages, but not enough to report reliable averages for small subgroups within each state. SABDB is a Bayesian model I developed that borrows information across states only as much as the data say those states are alike. Move the sliders to change how the states differ and see how SABDB compares with standard approaches.

Paper

Small Area Bayesian Dynamic Borrowing: Adaptive Subgroup Estimation for Large-Scale Educational Assessments

Sinan Yavuz & David Kaplan · arXiv:2608.07708 · 2026

NAEP suppresses subgroup estimates when fewer than 62 students are sampled, which falls hardest on historically underrepresented groups. The paper compares SABDB with unit-level HBSAE in a simulation modeled on NAEP grade 8 mathematics and a PISA 2018 case study. SABDB achieved near-nominal interval coverage in both, while HBSAE's intervals were narrow but badly miscalibrated.

Direct sample mean

Average of the sampled students in each state and subgroup. Unbiased, but very noisy when only a handful of students are sampled, and undefined when fewer than two are.

HBSAE standard practice

Hierarchical model with a random intercept per state and subgroup effects shared by all states. Very stable, but it assumes every state has the same subgroup gaps.

SABDB this research

Each state gets its own intercept and its own subgroup gaps, drawn from a shared distribution. A separate variance τ² per coefficient lets the data decide how much each one borrows.

SD of state-specific subgroup gaps, in NAEP points. HBSAE assumes 0.

More states give the models more information about how states vary.

About this demo

Each replication simulates a population of states with four subgroups (approximate 2019 NAEP grade 8 math means), draws a sample of students from every state, and fits all three methods. SABDB and HBSAE use the same priors as the Stan models in the SABDB repository. Here they're fit with a Gibbs sampler in your browser (700 iterations, 250 warm-up) instead of Stan's HMC.

To keep it fast, the demo simplifies the dissertation's simulation design: students are sampled at random within each state rather than through schools, there are no sampling weights, and within-subgroup SD is fixed at 36 points. Results here illustrate how the methods behave. They aren't the dissertation's reported results.
